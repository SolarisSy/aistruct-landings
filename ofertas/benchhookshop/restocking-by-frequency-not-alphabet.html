Restocking by Frequency, Not Alphabet

Apr 9, 2026 · Benchhook · Reading time about seven minutes

The first wall I painted was sorted by family. Chisels together, in width order. Screwdrivers together, flat then cross. Pliers in a neat row of three. It looked like a catalogue page, and for about six weeks I was very pleased with it. Then I noticed something ugly: I was walking. Not far — the shop is six square metres — but constantly, in short annoyed hops between the vise and the far end of the board, because the 6 mm chisel I used forty times a week lived at the end of a row that started with the 25 mm one I used twice a year. The wall was tidy. The wall was also wrong.

Tidiness is a property of the picture, not the work

Alphabetical order, size order, family order — all three are sorting rules that make the board easy to photograph. They optimise for the moment you stand back and look at it. Frequency order optimises for the other three hundred moments, the ones where your hands are full and you want the thing you always want.

The reason family sorting feels right is that it matches how the tools were sold to you, and how a catalogue lists them. But a catalogue is an index. A wall is a workspace. An index answers "where is the 10 mm one" — a question you rarely have, because you can see the whole board at once. A workspace answers "get me the thing I reach for" — a question you have all day. Sorting by name solves a problem the wall had already solved by being visible in the first place, which is the same argument I make about why the drawer hides losses: a surface that shows everything doesn't need an alphabet.

Three zones, and what earns each one

I ended up with three bands rather than a strict ranking, because a strict ranking implies a precision I don't have. The bands are defined by body movement, not by distance in centimetres.

The fourth row matters more than the first three. A wall with no exit rule fills up, and a full wall stops being readable — the outlines crowd, the gaps get harder to see, and the end-of-day glance stops working. Something has to leave for something to arrive.

A tool doesn't own its hook. It rents it, and the rent is paid in use.

The awkward part: the first layout is a guess

Here is the thing nobody says out loud when they recommend frequency sorting. You cannot sort by frequency until you know the frequency, and you don't know the frequency until you've watched yourself work. Everyone thinks they know. Almost nobody does. I would have sworn the mallet was my most-used object; it turned out to sit behind the marking knife and the rule by a wide margin, and it wasn't close.

So the first layout is fiction. Treat it that way. Put the tools where you believe they belong, accept that a good share of those beliefs are wrong, and build in a way of finding out. The mistake isn't guessing — you have to start somewhere. The mistake is treating the guess as a decision and then screwing the holders down permanently to defend it.

Watching without a clipboard. A tally sheet on the wall dies in three days; nobody stops mid-cut to write. What survives is marking the hook itself. Keep a soft pencil hanging by the board and put one small tick beside the outline when you take the tool down. One tick, no date, no note. After a month the ticks cluster, and the cluster is your real layout — not the one you imagined.

A season of ticks, then move things

I run the review with the seasons, roughly, because that's the natural rhythm of what a small shop makes. Four times a year is often enough to catch a real shift and rare enough that you don't fidget with the wall instead of working.

  • Read the ticks, not your memory. Count them per outline. The top handful goes to arm's reach whether or not that feels elegant.
  • Look for zero. An outline with no ticks all season is a candidate for one walk. Two seasons at zero and it comes off the wall entirely.
  • Watch for pairs. Two tools that are almost always taken down together should be neighbours, even if their tick counts differ. The knife and the small square are one gesture, not two.
  • Respect the awkward truth. Sometimes a beautiful tool has three ticks and an ugly one has sixty. The ugly one wins the good hook.
  • Wipe and start again. Fresh ticks each season, otherwise you're reading history instead of the present.

Then move things. Actually move them. A review that produces no movement usually means you didn't read the ticks honestly, or the season was too quiet to say anything — which is itself information, and a reason to wait rather than to reshuffle for the sake of it.

Why the outlines are paint, and why that's the whole point

If the layout is going to change four times a year, the marking system has to be cheap to undo. That's the practical argument for painted silhouettes over engraved plates, moulded holders, or anything bought as a permanent fitting. Paint is a sentence you can rewrite. A router-cut holder is a sentence carved into the desk.

A repaint costs a few minutes and a scrap of sandpaper. Moving a purpose-made holder costs new screw holes in a board that only has so many, plus the small internal argument about whether the money you spent on it should keep the tool where it is. That argument is the real cost — a permanent fixture doesn't just hold a tool, it holds an opinion you had last year. This is a version of the same trade-off I worked through in painted outlines versus labels, where the fixed thing wins on neatness and loses on everything else.

There's a second-order benefit. When the wall is known to be provisional, you stop resisting the evidence. A layout you can change in ten minutes is a layout you'll actually change when the ticks say to. A layout that cost you a Saturday of careful joinery is a layout you'll defend against your own data, and quietly keep walking those extra steps for another year.

Frequency has a shape, and the shape drifts

Use isn't constant. A run of drawer work pulls the small saw and the marking gauge to the front for a month. A batch of finishing pushes them back and brings the scrapers forward. If you only ever sort once, you're sorting for the work you happened to be doing that week, forever.

The seasonal review handles the slow drift. The fast drift — a two-week project with unusual demands — I handle differently: those tools sit loose on the bench for the duration and go back to their proper hooks at the end. The wall stays the baseline. The bench absorbs the exception. Mixing those up is how a board slowly turns into a pile, and the count at the end of the day is what catches it before it does.

A note on scale

This is one person's arrangement in a six-square-metre room, written up because the reasoning may be useful, not because it's a standard. A shared shop, a shop with more floor, or one where two people reach for the same rule at once will produce different zones and a different exit rule. Take the method — guess, watch, move — rather than the specific placements. Your ticks will not look like mine.
