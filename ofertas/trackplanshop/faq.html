Frequently asked

Questions we get asked

From readers planning their first spare-room layout to long-time operators debating radius over email. If your question isn't here, write to us.

What's the best scale for a small spare room?

There isn't a single best answer — it depends on how much running length you want versus how much detail you want to see up close. Smaller scales like N generally fit more railway into the same floor space; larger scales like HO or OO show more detail but ask for tighter curves or a smaller track plan to compensate.

How tight can a curve be before it causes problems?

It depends on your stock. Short wheelbase equipment tolerates tighter curves than long passenger cars or large steam locomotives. A curve that's technically "minimum radius" for your scale can still look cramped or cause coupler issues with longer stock, so it's worth testing with your actual equipment before committing benchwork to it.

Do I need a siding if I'm not planning to shunt?

Not necessarily. A siding that exists purely to look busy but never gets used in a session is just extra track to maintain. If you don't plan to operate switching moves, a siding is optional scenery rather than a functional necessity.

What's the difference between a fiddle yard and a staging yard?

They're generally the same idea under two names — off-scene track used to store or swap trains before they re-enter the visible layout. "Fiddle yard" is the more common British term; "staging yard" is the more common North American one.

How do I start writing a timetable for my layout?

Start smaller than feels natural — two or three trains with a clear job each, run in a fixed order, is usually more satisfying than a dense schedule you can't keep up with. Once that feels comfortable, add trains gradually and see where the schedule starts to strain.

Is DCC necessary for timetable operation?

No. Timetable operation is about the schedule and the paperwork, not the control system — plenty of layouts run a full timetable on DC block control. DCC makes some things more convenient, particularly independent control of multiple locomotives, but it isn't a requirement.

How many trains can a small layout realistically run in a session?

Fewer than most people expect at first. A layout with one or two sidings and a short main line often supports three to six meaningful movements in an hour once switching time is accounted for — trying to run more than that tends to feel rushed rather than satisfying.

Do I need signals to run a timetable?

No, but they can add structure. A simple set of rules — a signal at danger means stop, a siding entrance is only cleared when it's empty — gives operators something concrete to follow, even on a layout with just a few signals rather than a full block system.

How is Trackplan different from a manufacturer's blog?

We're not affiliated with any manufacturer and we don't review or sell products. Trackplan is written by hobbyists about the decisions involved in planning and operating a layout — track plans, sidings, benchwork and timetables — not about what to buy.

Does Trackplan sell anything?

No. The journal is free to read, and Timetable Board, our companion planning app, is a separate tool we mention where relevant. We don't run product reviews or sponsored posts.

What is Timetable Board and do I need it to use the site?

Timetable Board is our companion app for building session paperwork — waybills, switch lists and a session clock — from a track plan you enter. You don't need it to read anything on Trackplan; it's a separate tool for readers who want to plan an operating session.

How often does Trackplan publish?

Roughly every two to three weeks. We'd rather publish something considered than keep a strict schedule, so timing varies with what the editors are actually building and testing.

Can I submit my own track plan or layout story?

Yes — we're always glad to hear from readers. Write to hello@trackplan.shop with a description of your layout or plan and we'll follow up if it's a good fit for the journal.
