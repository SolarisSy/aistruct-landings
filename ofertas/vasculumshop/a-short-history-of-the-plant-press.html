A Short History of the Plant Press

From folded paper under a rock to the slatted wooden frame still sold today — how the tool settled on a shape and stayed there.

Field Note Lattice frame, maker unrecorded Strap buckles replaced; wood original

Open a supply catalog from the early 1900s next to one printed this year, and the plant press pictured in each is, give or take a strap buckle, the same object: two wooden lattice frames, blotting paper and corrugated ventilators sandwiched between them, cinched tight with webbing or a pair of bolts and wingnuts. Almost nothing else a field botanist carried has survived into ordinary use this unchanged — not the collecting tins, not the notebooks. The press did, because early collectors solved the problem quickly, and every attempt to improve on it since has run into the same three constraints: airflow, even pressure, and a frame light enough to carry into a field.

Before There Was a Frame

The earliest known method for keeping a plant flat and dry required no dedicated tool at all. Sixteenth-century naturalists are generally credited with the first deliberate herbarium sheets — a plant laid between the pages of a large book, the book itself doing double duty as weight and drying chamber, sometimes with a stone set on top. It worked, after a fashion, but slowly and unevenly. A book has no airflow through its pages, so moisture had nowhere to go except sideways into whatever paper sat nearby, and a specimen pressed this way could take weeks to dry, if it dried true at all rather than staining the pages around it.

Traveling collectors of the next two centuries improvised around the same limitation — sheets of paper stacked between wooden boards, weighted with rocks at camp or lashed with rope. The insight that would eventually define the tool, that a plant needs moving air as much as pressure, took a surprisingly long time to arrive, mostly because nobody needed a portable version until expedition botany made portability the whole point.

The Frame Finds Its Slats

By the age of long sea voyages and overland expeditions, the requirements had changed. A collector filling sheets by the hundred needed something that could be strapped shut in the field, stacked in a hold or saddlebag, and opened again days later without the contents having rotted. The slatted wooden frame — two lattices of thin wood strips, open enough to let air pass straight through the stack rather than just sideways — answered that directly. Corrugated ventilators between the blotters did the rest, carrying air the width of the press instead of letting it pool against the paper.

The design spread quickly because it had almost no failure mode left to fix. Solid boards were tried and abandoned — they held pressure well but starved the interior of air, exactly backward from what a drying plant needs. Metal frames appeared later and mostly failed to catch on outside laboratories, since wood is lighter, resists dampness better, and can be repaired in the field with a pocketknife and a length of cord. See What Humidity Does to a Press for the airflow logic that made the lattice non-negotiable in the first place.

Herbaria That Outlived Their Collectors

Pressed specimens, properly dried and labeled, last a very long time — long enough that sheets prepared by eighteenth-century collectors are still consulted by researchers now, brittle at the edges but readable. A handful of institutional herbaria grew out of the personal collections of individual naturalists, expanded over generations by donation and exchange until they held millions of sheets, each a small, dated record of a plant that grew in a specific place at a specific time.

A herbarium sheet is one of the few objects a person can make in an afternoon that a stranger might still be reading in two hundred years.

Some of the collections that trace back furthest include:

  • University and academy herbaria, built from eighteenth-century systematists' working collections and later formalized into research archives.
  • Expedition herbaria assembled by ship's naturalists, whose sheets sometimes carry the only surviving record of a landscape since changed.
  • Regional herbaria at botanical gardens, grown as much from amateur donations as from professional collecting trips.
  • University teaching herbaria, smaller in scale but continuously added to — often where a beginner's first properly labeled sheet ends up.

None of that longevity depends on a fancy tool. It depends on a flat, evenly dried specimen and a legible label — see Writing a Label That Lasts for what makes a sheet worth keeping. The press just has to get the plant there without damaging it.

Why the Shape Never Changed

It is tempting to assume a tool this old is simply waiting for modernization, but the slatted frame has survived roughly two centuries of chances to be replaced because the underlying physics never changed. A plant still needs the same three things a nineteenth-century press provided: pressure to hold tissue flat while it dries, open structure to carry moisture away rather than trap it, and a frame sturdy enough to stay strapped tight without warping. Straps became nylon instead of leather; bolts sometimes replaced buckles. The lattice is, structurally, the same answer to the same three demands.

What actually changed: mostly the materials at the margins — synthetic webbing, machine-cut slats, printed blotting paper — not the geometry. The open lattice, the stacked ventilators, and the even clamping pressure are the same three elements a collector two centuries ago would recognize on sight.

What a Modern Press Still Owes the Old One

Laid side by side, a contemporary press and a period one differ mostly in finish, not function — as the table below shows.

The lesson is a little humbling for anyone drawn to gadgetry: the collectors who first got the geometry right did so through trial and long field seasons, not special access to materials modern makers lack. It is a rare case of a design reaching good-enough early and never needing to leave it. This article is a general historical overview written for background interest, not a definitive institutional history. Dates and attributions in the early record of pressing technique vary between sources, and readers researching a specific collector or herbarium should consult that institution's own archival documentation.
