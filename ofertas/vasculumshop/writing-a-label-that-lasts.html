Writing a Label That Lasts

Six lines of information turn a pressed plant into a record. Anything less, and a good specimen becomes an unlabeled scrap in a box.

A specimen with a beautifully pressed plant and no label is, in practical terms, worth less than the blank sheet it's mounted on. Someone will eventually open the folder — a student doing inventory, an heir clearing out a relative's study, a volunteer digitizing a backlog nobody's touched in years — and find a flat, colorful, unreadable object. The plant did its part by staying flat and keeping something close to its living color. The label is the part that makes any of that effort mean something to a person who wasn't in the field the day it was collected. Writing one well takes under a minute. Knowing what belongs on it is the part most people skip.

What the label is actually doing

A pressed specimen only has value in relation to where, when, and by whom it was collected. Two sheets of the same species, mounted identically, are not interchangeable — one might document a population that's since been paved over, the other a plant from a garden three streets from where it claims to be wild. The plant tissue itself can be re-examined for decades after pressing: morphology checked against a newer key, a fragment sampled for later comparison, pollen counted under a scope. None of that re-examination means anything without the label attached, because the label is the only thing tying the physical object to a real place and a real moment. A photograph can be mislabeled just as easily as a plant; the slip of paper glued or pinned to the sheet is what a collection actually trusts.

The six lines, in order

Every convention I've come across, from a university herbarium's printed template to a handwritten card tucked into a field notebook, comes back to the same six pieces of information. Leave one out and the specimen gets harder to use. Leave out more than one, and it stops being data and starts being decoration.

  • Determination — the scientific name, plus who assigned it and when. Not just "aster."
  • Locality — a place specific enough that a stranger with a map could stand roughly where you stood.
  • Date collected — the day the plant came out of the ground, not the day it was pressed or mounted.
  • Habitat — sun or shade, soil or rock, elevation, what else was growing nearby.
  • Collector — a full name, not initials that mean nothing outside your own notebook.
  • Collector's number — a sequential number tied to your own running list, not the sheet's position in a folder.

The collector's number does more work than its short line suggests. It's the thread that connects a herbarium sheet to whatever else survives from that day — a field notebook entry, a duplicate mailed to another herbarium, a photograph taken before pressing. Anyone who has tried to reconstruct a trip from a notebook kept without a numbering system knows how fast that thread disappears once the sequence breaks.

Determination is a claim, not a fact

The determination line is often written with more confidence than it deserves. Taxonomy moves; a genus split that seemed settled a decade ago gets revised again, and a name that was correct when the label was written can become outdated without the specimen itself changing at all. Older herbaria handle this by adding annotation slips below the original label rather than rewriting it — a small strip of paper recording the new name, who proposed it, and when, stacked on top of the original determination like a running edit history. The specimen accumulates opinions instead of erasing them, which is part of what makes a heavily annotated old label worth reading slowly rather than taking at face value.

A label doesn't have to be right forever. It has to say, honestly, who thought it was right, and when.

Where labels go wrong

Most ruined labels aren't illegible from age — they were incomplete from the day they were written. A handful of patterns show up often enough to be worth naming directly:

  • Writing the common name only, which drifts by region and decade and eventually points to nothing.
  • Recording the date the sheet was mounted instead of the date the plant was collected, sometimes weeks apart.
  • "Habitat: field" or "roadside," with nothing about soil, light, or elevation to distinguish it from a thousand other roadsides.
  • "Same locality as above," written on a sheet that later gets separated from whatever "above" referred to.

Before you close the folder

Write in permanent black ink or a 2H–4H pencil on acid-free paper. Ballpoint smears against the sheet stacked on top of it in a press or a box, and cheap ink can fade to a gray ghost long before the drawer is opened again.

A quick reference

If you're standing at the desk with a fresh specimen and a blank label, this is the shortest version of everything above.

Line What it should say What gets left out
Determination Genus, species, determiner's name, date determined Who made the call, and when
Locality County or region, distance and direction from a named landmark Anything specific enough to relocate
Date collected The day the plant left the ground Confused with the mounting date
Habitat Light, substrate, elevation, associated species Anything beyond "field" or "woods"
Collector & number Full name, personal sequential number Initials only, or no number at all

Writing it so a stranger can use it

The real test for a finished label isn't whether you can read it — it's whether someone with zero context, twenty years from now, could read it and know exactly what they're holding. That means no abbreviations that only make sense in your own head, no locality description that depends on a road that may not exist by then, and ink or pencil that won't have faded to nothing by the time anyone looks again. Write the label before the plant leaves the press, while the details are still accurate in memory, and mount it low enough on the sheet that it never covers a leaf, a flower, or anything else someone might need to examine later.

A note on this piece

The six-line format described here is a personal habit built up over years of pressing, not a single official standard. Herbaria, universities, and individual collectors keep their own label conventions, often close to this one but rarely identical. Treat it as a floor to build from, not a rule to enforce on someone else's sheets.
