Storing a Herbarium at Home

A shoebox works for a season. A collection meant to outlast you needs acid-free folders, a dry shelf, and a plan for silverfish.

Field Note Genus folders, closet shelf Fourteen years in, no foxing yet

Most pressed-plant collections die of neglect long after the pressing itself was finished well. The flattening was careful, the color held, the label was written the same week the plant came out of the press — and then the sheet went into a manila folder in a desk drawer, and ten years later it came out brittle at the edges, freckled with rust-colored spots, and smelling faintly of the drawer rather than the plant. None of that damage happened during collection. It happened during storage, slowly, in conditions nobody was watching. A herbarium meant to last is not a display; it is an archive, and archives have different rules than the rest of a house.

Why the folder matters more than the box

The single biggest storage mistake is reaching for whatever paper is on hand — a manila folder, a scrapbook page, the cardboard insert from a shirt box. Most of that paper is acidic, and acid migrates. Over years, it travels from the folder into the specimen sheet, then into the plant tissue itself, and the result is the yellow-brown staining collectors call foxing: small, spreading spots that have nothing to do with mold and everything to do with chemistry. Acid-free (buffered, lignin-free) folders and boxes cost more than the alternative, but they are the one material choice in this entire hobby that cannot be substituted later without unloading and reloading every sheet.

A basic setup is three layers: the specimen mounted on stiff paper (see Mounting Without Glue for how to get it there without adhesive that yellows), a single acid-free folder holding one or two sheets, and an archival box sized to hold the folders upright, spine-down, like books rather than stacked flat like pancakes. Stacking flat under weight is how corners crush and petals shatter years after the plant itself has nothing left to give.

Where the shelf goes

Light and heat do the same slow damage as acid, just by a different route — they break down pigment and embrittle the paper fiber. A closet shelf, an interior cabinet, or a drawer in a room that doesn't swing wildly in temperature all work better than anything near a window or above a radiator. Basements are a mixed bag: cooler is good, but many basements run damp, and damp is worse than almost anything else on this list.

The target most collections aim for is a room that stays reasonably cool and dry year-round, out of direct sun, with some air movement rather than a sealed, airless box. A closed cabinet with the door left slightly ajar, or a box with a few small vents, beats a box sealed tight enough to trap whatever moisture is already inside it.

The box protects the sheet from light and dust. It cannot protect the sheet from the humidity already trapped inside it.

Humidity, the quiet threat

A herbarium sheet that has gone properly through the press is dry enough to store, but "dry enough to store" is not "dry forever." Paper and plant tissue are both mildly hygroscopic — they pull moisture back out of humid air over time, especially in a house that runs warm and damp through a summer. That reabsorbed moisture is what eventually invites mold, and it is also what softens a specimen enough for it to shift and crack inside its folder every time the box is handled.

Where reliable dehumidification isn't practical, a simple silica gel packet tucked into the storage box — replaced or recharged a few times a year — does a lot of the work quietly. Checking a handful of sheets by touch once a season, especially after a humid stretch, catches a problem while it's still just a slightly limp folder and not yet a spotted specimen.

Pest check: silverfish and certain small beetles will eat dried plant tissue and the paste in old mounts without hesitation. A quick look under folders every few months, and keeping the storage area free of food debris and standing cardboard, prevents most infestations before they start — a freezer cycle for new or secondhand specimens (several days at a hard freeze, sealed in a bag) is a common precaution before anything joins an existing collection.

Organizing so the collection stays usable

A herbarium that can't be searched isn't much different from a box of pressed leaves. Most home collections settle on one of two logics: alphabetical by family, then genus within it, or straight alphabetical by genus if the collection is small enough that families don't add much value yet. Either works — what matters is picking one and sticking to it, because a specimen filed under the wrong logic is functionally lost until someone reshuffles the whole box.

  • One folder per species is the cleanest unit — it keeps duplicate collections of the same plant together without crowding a folder meant for something else.
  • A running index card or simple list at the front of each box (family, genus, species, sheet count) saves a search through every folder later.
  • Leave growing room. A box packed to its exact current capacity means the next specimen has nowhere to go without reshuffling everything behind it.
  • Keep the label consistent with the filing system — see Writing a Label That Lasts for what a label needs to carry so a specimen still means something decades from now.

What a shoebox collection eventually needs to become

Almost every serious herbarium starts as a shoebox, and there's nothing wrong with that for a first season of collecting. The point at which it needs a real system is usually somewhere between fifty and a hundred sheets, when finding a specific specimen starts taking longer than it should and the folder is bulging past what the lid can close over cleanly. That's the moment to migrate into acid-free folders and a proper filing order — doing it early, before the collection outgrows a single afternoon's work, is far easier than doing it after years of accumulation. It also pays to think ahead about which specimens are genuinely difficult to keep flat in the first place; see The Species That Refuse to Flatten for how those cases change the storage math too.

This article covers general archival storage practices for home plant collections and is not a substitute for the specific guidance of an institutional herbarium or conservator, particularly for specimens of scientific or historical value.
