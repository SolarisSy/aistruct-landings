The Blotter-Change Interval

The single habit that separates a flat, true-colored sheet from a brown, mildewed one — and how to tell when the paper is actually done absorbing.

Field Note Blotter stack, day two Still damp at the corolla — not ready

Ask anyone who has opened a press three weeks late what a ruined specimen looks like, and they will not describe a broken stem or a curled leaf. They will describe a smell — sweetish, a little like wet cardboard — on a bloom that has gone the color of tea. The plant did not fail. The blotter did, or rather nobody changed it in time, and a sheet of paper that had already given up its job kept sitting there anyway, quietly turning a specimen into something no herbarium wants. The blotter-change interval is the least glamorous discipline in pressing, and the one that decides almost everything else.

What the blotter is actually doing

A blotting sheet is not a passive layer between your plant and the press boards. It is the only thing pulling water out of the tissue. The corrugated cardboard and the wooden slats supply airflow and pressure; the blotter itself absorbs. Once it is saturated, it stops working — it becomes, in effect, a wet compress sitting against your specimen, holding humidity in exactly the place you were trying to remove it from. A blotter that has done its job for two days and is left in place for five is not neutral. It is actively working against you.

This is why "how long do I press a plant" is the wrong question. The right one is "how long does this blotter stay dry enough to keep absorbing," and that answer changes by the hour depending on what is inside the press and what the air outside it is doing.

Reading a blotter by feel, not by the calendar

Pull a blotter out and press the back of your hand flat against the center — not the edge, which dries first and will lie to you. A dry blotter feels cool but not damp, with a faint papery stiffness, almost like a stack of unopened mail. A blotter that still has work to do feels slightly limp, cooler than room temperature, and it will often show a darker outline exactly where the thickest part of the plant sits — the stem base, a flower head, a fleshy leaf midrib. That shadow is the map of where moisture is still moving.

Some collectors weigh the blotter dry, then weigh it again at each change, watching the number close in on the baseline — a good habit for a reference collection, though most manage fine on feel alone once they have done it a dozen times.

The blotter is not there to hold the plant flat. Gravity and the press boards do that. The blotter's only job is to leave with the water.

A rough schedule, by thickness and climate

There is no universal number of days — a fern frond in a dry inland climate behaves nothing like a succulent leaf in a coastal one — but a working baseline helps, and you adjust from there by feel. See What Humidity Does to a Press for how ambient conditions shift these numbers further.

In a humid climate or a rainy stretch, halve those intervals for the first two changes — the paper simply cannot hold what the air keeps giving back. In a dry, low-humidity room, you can often stretch to the longer end without penalty, since the surrounding air is doing part of the blotter's job for it.

Rule of thumb: change blotters daily for the first two days on anything with real bulk — a flower head, a fleshy stem, a folded leaf. After that, the interval can stretch as the specimen approaches equilibrium with the paper, but never guess past four days without checking.

What happens when you skip a change

The consequences show up in a fairly predictable order. First comes discoloration — greens shift toward brown or black at the thickest points, because tissue sitting in trapped moisture oxidizes rather than dries cleanly. Next comes incomplete flattening: a specimen that stalls halfway through drying often relaxes slightly when finally removed, springing back just enough to warp against the sheet later. Worst is mildew, which needs surprisingly little time — a fully saturated blotter left against fleshy tissue for three or four extra days in a warm room is enough to start it, and once it has taken hold, the smell and the specimen are both gone for good. None of this is dramatic to watch happen; it is just a paper towel doing nothing while you assume it is doing something.

Building the habit around a schedule you'll actually keep

  • Set the first change for 24 hours after pressing, no exceptions — this is when the bulk of the water moves.
  • Check by feel at every change, not just by counting days; humidity swings will move your interval around.
  • Keep a running note of what you pressed and when you changed it — a pattern for your own climate emerges after four or five presses. See Writing a Label That Lasts for how that same habit of dated notes carries through to the finished specimen.
  • Have a spare set of dry blotters ready before you start — nothing derails a change like discovering the replacement paper is also damp.

Knowing when to stop

The paper tells you this too, eventually. A specimen is done when the blotter comes away completely dry and stays that way overnight — no cool patch, no shadow, no give under your palm. Some collectors add a final 24-hour check with a fresh, room-temperature blotter to confirm nothing is still migrating from a thick vein or stem base. It costs one day and removes most of the guesswork.

This article is for general reference on drying technique and does not replace judgment specific to your own climate, plant material, or press setup. Conditions vary enough between rooms and seasons that any schedule here should be treated as a starting point, adjusted by what your own blotters tell you.
