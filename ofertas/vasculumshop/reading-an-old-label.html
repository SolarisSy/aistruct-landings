Reading an Old Label

Faded ink, an abbreviated county name, a collector's initials instead of a name — notes on decoding a specimen someone else pressed decades ago.

Archive Slip Undated determination, second hand Co. abbreviation unresolved — cross-check pending

A shoebox turns up in a relative's attic, or a folder surfaces in a university basement on its way to being digitized, and inside is a pressed plant older than anyone currently working in the building. The plant, if it was pressed with any care at all, has generally held up fine — flat, faded but recognizable, strapped or glued to a sheet that yellowed evenly over the decades. The label taped beside it is a different problem entirely. Three lines of cramped cursive in ink gone the color of weak tea, a county cut down to four letters nobody uses that way anymore, and a name that turns out, on close inspection, to be initials only. Reading that label — actually reading it, not just admiring the penmanship — takes a different kind of attention than pressing ever did, and it rewards patience the plant itself no longer needs.

What you're actually holding

An old label is rarely a single, tidy statement. It is more often a stack, physically or informally — the original slip in one hand, an annotation added below it decades later in a second hand, sometimes a third slip pinned on top disputing the second. The six-line convention described in Writing a Label That Lasts assumes a single, current label written once and trusted from then on. An inherited sheet almost never gives you that. It gives you a sequence, and the order matters: the bottom layer is the oldest claim, and each addition above it is someone revising, correcting, or simply restating what came before with more confidence than the evidence necessarily earned.

Before interpreting anything, transcribe what's there literally — every abbreviation, every illegible stroke marked as illegible rather than guessed at. It is tempting to fill gaps with what seems plausible. Resist that until the locality and the determination have both been checked against something outside the label itself.

The abbreviation problem

Older labels lean hard on a shorthand that was once universal in field botany and is now mostly unfamiliar. Leg. — short for legit — names who collected the plant, not necessarily who wrote the label. Det. marks a determination, often added years after collection and frequently in a different hand altogether. S.n. means sine numero, no collector's number assigned, which was common practice on casual or amateur sheets long before numbering became standard. Ex hort. — ex horto, from a garden — is the one worth catching every time, because it flags a cultivated origin rather than a wild collection, and mistaking the two turns a garden escapee into what looks like a genuine range record.

County abbreviations cause a different kind of trouble. "Wash. Co." could sit in dozens of states, and a label rarely repeats the state on every line once it's been written once at the top of a batch. If the state isn't obvious from context, don't assume — check the collector's known range of activity first, since most collectors worked a fairly consistent home territory across their careers.

Handwriting that has drifted out of use

Letterforms move over a century the same way spoken language does, just more slowly and less noticeably while it's happening. A numeral 7 with a crossbar can read as a 1 to modern eyes; a capital cursive G from an early-1900s hand can look closer to a modern S; a lowercase looped L and a lowercase looped E get confused constantly in labels written in a hurry. Iron gall ink, common well into the twentieth century, oxidizes to a rust brown that can look uniformly faded at a glance but actually varies stroke by stroke depending on how much ink loaded the pen at that instant — which sometimes makes a letter that looks missing simply thin rather than absent.

The most reliable fix isn't squinting harder at the one letter in question. It's comparing that letter against every other instance of the same letter elsewhere on the same label, in the same hand. A collector's capital "M" in their signature tells you what their capital "M" looks like everywhere else they wrote one.

An old label isn't illegible until you've compared every letter you can't read against a letter you can, in the same hand, in the same ink.

Cross-referencing a locality that no longer exists

Place names on old labels have a way of quietly stopping being true. A township gets absorbed into a neighboring one, a rail stop used as a landmark closes and the name falls out of local memory, a county splits into two and the original name survives only on maps from before the split. None of this means the locality was wrong when it was written — it means the map underneath it changed while the label stayed still.

Working backward usually starts with the collection date. If a label cites a county that didn't yet exist on that date, the collector was almost certainly using the parent county's name, which was still standard practice at the time. Period gazetteers, historical county-boundary maps, and local historical society records can usually confirm a formation date in a few minutes, and that single check often resolves what looked like an error into a perfectly consistent record from an older map.

Cross-check tip: if a locality would represent a significant range extension for the species, treat that as a flag to re-read the label before treating it as a flag to update the range. A misread abbreviation is far more common than a genuinely unusual record, and the plain reading is worth a second look before either is accepted.

A working sequence for decoding a stack

None of the steps above are useful in isolation — they only work in a fixed order, since guessing at a locality before separating the layers, or interpreting an abbreviation before transcribing it literally, tends to compound one small uncertainty into a much larger one.

  • Separate the layers first, oldest at the bottom, and note which hand wrote which slip before reading any of them for content.
  • Transcribe literally before interpreting — mark anything genuinely illegible rather than filling it with a guess.
  • Resolve abbreviations against a reference list, not memory; the same short form meant different things at different institutions.
  • Check the locality against a period-appropriate map or gazetteer, not a current one, especially for county names.
  • Read the determination history as a sequence of claims rather than a single fact, and note who made each one and roughly when.

Most of this decoding happens on sheets that were kept loosely for years before anyone catalogued them properly — often exactly the kind of storage described in Storing a Herbarium at Home, where a box outlasts a cabinet by decades even if it treats the paper less kindly along the way.

A short reference for the common shorthand

These five turn up often enough on pre-1970s sheets to be worth memorizing rather than looking up every time.

AbbreviationFull formWhat it tells you
leg.legitNames the collector — not necessarily the hand that wrote the label
det.determinavitAn identification, often added much later and by a different person
s.n.sine numeroNo collector's number was assigned — common on casual or early sheets
s.d.sine dieNo date recorded, distinct from a date that's simply hard to read
ex hort.ex hortoCultivated origin, not a wild collection — check before citing as a range record

None of this shorthand was ever fully standardized across institutions, which is exactly why the fifth line in the sequence above — checking a reference rather than trusting memory — matters more than it seems like it should for five short abbreviations.

This article describes general conventions in historical herbarium labeling for reference purposes. Abbreviation practices, county boundaries, and handwriting habits varied considerably by region, era, and individual collector, and no single method resolves every label with certainty. Treat any reading offered here as a starting point for comparison against period-specific sources, not as a final determination on its own.
