The Species That Refuse to Flatten

Succulents, cones, and thick stems resist the press on principle. A few workarounds that don't involve giving up.

Field Note Sedum stem, split before pressing Bisected lengthwise, cut face down

Every collector eventually meets a specimen that will not lie down. Not a difficult specimen in the ordinary sense — not fussy about drying time or prone to shattering when handled dry — but one that is simply too much plant for the space between two boards. A jade cutting an inch thick. A pinecone that is, structurally, a sphere. A cactus pad that would rather rot from the inside than surrender its water in any orderly way. The instinct is to press harder, and the instinct is usually wrong. What these species need is not more pressure. It is less material standing between the boards and the paper, and that means cutting before you press, not after.

Why thickness defeats a press before weight ever does

A plant press works by pulling moisture out through capillary action into the blotters above and below the specimen, with the boards supplying even, distributed pressure to keep the tissue flat while that happens. The system assumes a more or less two-dimensional object: a leaf, a flattened flower, a thin stem section. Once a specimen gets much thicker than a few millimeters at its densest point, two things go wrong at once. The center of the tissue is now too far from either blotter surface for water to travel efficiently, so it stays wet long after the outer layers look dry. And the boards, unable to compress a solid mass the way they compress a leaf, either fail to flatten the specimen at all or crush it unevenly, damaging one side while barely touching the other. Neither outcome is fixable after the fact — it has to be addressed before the specimen goes anywhere near the press.

The case for splitting the specimen first

The workaround most experienced pressers land on is unglamorous: cut the thick part in half, lengthwise, before it ever touches a blotter. A jade or sedum stem sliced down its long axis exposes the pith to air and to the blotter directly, cutting the effective drying distance by half or more. A small pinecone, halved through its axis with a sharp blade, opens every scale to airflow instead of trapping moisture at the core. A cactus pad, scored or thinly sliced rather than pressed whole, avoids the slow internal spoilage that whole succulent tissue tends toward under sustained pressure. The cut face goes down against the blotter, exposed side up if you want to preserve visible structure, and the two halves are pressed as a matched pair rather than one impossible lump.

This is not a technique invented for pressing — herbarium collectors have split thick rhizomes and fleshy material for well over a century, for exactly this reason. It tends to get rediscovered by anyone who tries to press a whole agave rosette and watches the center darken over two weeks while the edges look perfectly fine.

The plant does not need to survive intact. The record of it does.

What to cut, and how much

Not every thick specimen needs a full bisection. A short list of what tends to need what:

  • Succulent leaves and stems — split lengthwise through the center, or shave a thin longitudinal slice if you want to keep one half looking whole for display.
  • Cones and woody fruiting bodies — halve through the long axis with a fine saw or sharp blade; pressing whole almost always ends in a specimen that never fully dries at the core.
  • Thick fleshy stems (cacti, some succulents, some vines) — score deeply or slice into sections no thicker than a pencil, laid flat rather than stacked.
  • Bulbous or tuberous bases — a single clean cut through the widest point, pressed cut-side down against a heavier blotter stack.

The general target is a cross-section no thicker than what a normal press could flatten without special handling — roughly the thickness of two or three stacked coins. Anything past that point tends to benefit from cutting rather than forcing.

Pressing the halves without losing the record

Once split, treat each half as its own specimen within the same folder. Lay them cut-side down against a slightly heavier blotter stack than usual, since the exposed tissue releases water faster at first and needs somewhere for it to go. Expect a longer total drying time than a comparable leaf, even after cutting — thick material still holds more water per square inch than it appears to, and changing the blotters more frequently in the first two or three days matters more here than almost anywhere else. See The Blotter-Change Interval for how to judge that timing by feel rather than by guesswork.

It also helps to keep a quick sketch or a phone photo of the specimen before cutting, since a bisected cone or a halved succulent stem can look unfamiliar once flattened and dried. That reference, tucked into the eventual folder alongside the label, saves a lot of second-guessing later when you or someone else tries to make sense of what's on the sheet.

A note on labeling split specimens: record that the material was bisected before pressing, and roughly how thick the original was. Twenty years from now, that detail is the difference between a puzzling flat cross-section and a specimen anyone can read correctly. More on what a label needs to carry in Writing a Label That Lasts.

Accepting an imperfect flatten

Some material will never lie fully flat, and that's worth accepting rather than fighting. A halved cone still has some dimensionality to it; a sliced cactus pad may retain a slight curve. The goal for these specimens is not a perfectly two-dimensional sheet but a stable, dry, legible record — something that can sit in a folder for decades without warping, discoloring, or drawing mildew. A slightly domed pinecone half that is thoroughly dry and well labeled is a far better specimen than a whole one that looked flat on day three and turned dark by day ten. If you're weighing how much material to bring in at all before you start cutting anything, Collecting Without a Plan covers the earlier decision of what's worth the effort.

This article describes general preparation technique for pressing thick or fleshy plant material and is offered for reference only. It is not a substitute for judgment specific to a particular species, tool, or collection purpose, and readers should use appropriate care when cutting plant material with sharp tools.
