Frequently asked questions

Questions readers send most often, about pressing plants and about the journal itself.

How long does a specimen actually need to stay in the press?

It depends on the plant's water content and your climate, but most thin-leaved specimens are dry within one to two weeks with regular blotter changes. Thick stems, cones, and succulent material can take three weeks or more. The specimen is done when it feels stiff and papery rather than cool or flexible.

Do I need a wooden press, or will books work?

Heavy books can flatten a specimen in a pinch, but they don't allow air to move through the stack the way a slatted or ventilated press does, so drying takes longer and mildew risk goes up. A basic wooden press with cardboard ventilators is inexpensive and worth the switch if you collect more than occasionally.

What's the minimum information a label needs?

Species name (or "unidentified"), locality specific enough to relocate the site, date of collection, collector's name, and habitat notes. Anything less and the specimen loses most of its value as a record, even if it looks perfect.

Can I press flowers and keep their color?

Some color always fades, but fast drying helps — silica gel or frequent blotter changes in the first few days tend to preserve more pigment than slow, humid pressing. Reds and yellows generally hold better than blues and purples.

Is it okay to collect from a roadside or a park?

Rules vary by jurisdiction and land manager. Many parks and protected areas prohibit collecting without a permit, while roadsides and private land (with permission) are often more flexible. Check local regulations before collecting, and never take the only individual of a species you find at a site.

How do I stop mildew from forming in the press?

Change blotters on schedule, keep the press somewhere dry and ventilated rather than a damp basement, and avoid pressing visibly wet or dew-covered material without blotting the excess moisture first.

What's the difference between mounting with glue and mounting with hinges?

Glue adheres the specimen permanently and can yellow or crack over decades. Hinges — small strips of gummed linen tape — hold the specimen at a few points and are reversible, which is why long-term collections favor them even though they take more care to apply.

Should I write directly on the specimen or only on the label?

Only on the label. Writing on plant tissue damages it and doesn't hold up over time; the label, mounted alongside the specimen, is the permanent record.

How do I store finished sheets so they last decades?

Acid-free folders inside acid-free boxes, kept flat, in a room with stable humidity and no direct sunlight. Cedar or a light insect deterrent helps keep silverfish and other pests away from both the paper and the plant material.

What's the point of keeping a herbarium if I'm not a botanist?

A personal herbarium is a record of where you've been and what was growing there at a specific time — useful for noticing change in a familiar place over years, and satisfying simply as a made object, independent of any scientific use.

Yes — write to hello@vasculum.shop. We're especially interested in regional variations on pressing timing, since climate changes the right answer more than most guides admit.
