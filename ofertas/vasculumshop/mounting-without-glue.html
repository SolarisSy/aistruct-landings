Mounting Without Glue

Adhesive yellows, glue lines crack, and future botanists will thank you for using linen tape and paper hinges instead.

Field Note Hinge and paper corner Specimen floats free of the sheet

Open an herbarium sheet from decades back and you sometimes find the plant lifting away clean, as if it had only ever been resting there. Open a different sheet from the same era and you find an amber halo instead — glue that has darkened, crept past the edge of the leaf it was meant to hold, and cracked into a fine web of lines under the light. Neither specimen failed. One was mounted to be handled, studied, and eventually removed if a taxonomist needed a closer look. The other was glued down for good, which turned out to mean glued down for the worse. The difference is not the plant. It is what was underneath it.

What glue does to a specimen over time

Most household and even many "craft" adhesives are built around synthetic polymers that were never tested for a hundred-year shelf life against plant tissue. They oxidize. They yellow. Some become brittle enough to crack along the stem the moment the sheet is flexed, taking a fragment of leaf with them. Worse, a specimen fixed in place with glue cannot be lifted for close examination, remounted if it's misidentified, or removed if the mounting paper itself needs replacing — and mounting paper does eventually need replacing. A permanent bond sounds like a virtue until you remember that a specimen's whole value is in being examined again, by someone who was not you, possibly decades from now.

The idea behind a hinge

A hinge or a paper corner does the opposite job: it holds the specimen in position without ever touching adhesive to the plant tissue itself. A hinge is a small folded strip, gummed on one side only, that straddles a sturdy part of the stem and is pasted to the backing sheet — not the plant. A corner is a small folded paper pocket, cut like a photo corner, that the stem or a leaf base simply slides into. Neither method requires the plant to bond to anything. The specimen sits in place, held by geometry and a light touch of tension rather than by chemistry, and it can be lifted out again in seconds with no residue and no damage. That reversibility is the entire point, and it is the same principle behind writing a label that lasts — record and mount for someone else's future use, not just your own present convenience.

A specimen glued down for good is only good until someone needs to see the other side of the stem.

Linen tape versus paper corners

Both methods show up in herbaria, and the choice mostly comes down to the shape of what you're mounting. A long, straight stem takes a linen hinge well — two or three short strips crossing it at intervals hold it flat without crushing it. A leaf base, a bulky flower head, or anything with an awkward pivot point often does better in a paper corner, which cradles rather than pins. Many working sheets use both on the same specimen: hinges along the stem, corners at the leaf tips, so nothing is under strain at a single point.

Paste, if you use it at all: a small dab of plain wheat starch paste under a hinge tab — never touching the specimen — is the one adhesive most conservation-minded mounters consider acceptable, because it stays water-soluble and reversible for decades rather than curing into a permanent, brittle bond.

Setting a hinge, in practice

  • Cut linen tape into short strips, roughly the width of the stem plus a margin on each side.
  • Dampen the gummed side lightly — a barely wet brush is enough — and press it across the stem so it wraps but doesn't compress the tissue.
  • Anchor each free end flat against the backing sheet, smoothing out air pockets before it dries.
  • For corners, fold a small triangle of archival paper, slit one edge, and slide the leaf base or petiole into the pocket before securing the corner's back to the sheet.
  • Leave the whole mount under light, even weight for a few hours so nothing lifts while the paste or gum sets.

The habit pairs naturally with everything else that keeps a specimen useful long after the press comes apart — the schedule behind the blotter-change interval gets the plant dry enough to mount cleanly in the first place, and a hinge only holds well on tissue that has actually finished drying.

Why this matters more than it looks like it should

A herbarium sheet is not really made for the person who presses it. It is made for whoever opens the folder next — a student confirming a species, a researcher tracing where a plant grew a century ago, a collector years later wondering what their own hand looked like when it was less practiced. Glue answers only the mounter's question: is it stuck down today. A hinge answers everyone else's question too: can this still be examined, safely, whenever it's needed. That's a small difference in technique and a large difference in what survives.

This article describes traditional mounting technique for personal and educational reference. It is not a substitute for the specific archival standards of any institution or collection, and materials should always be tested on a small, low-value specimen before use on anything irreplaceable.
