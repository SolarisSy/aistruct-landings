What Humidity Does to a Press

Too dry and the leaf shatters at the vein; too damp and it never really leaves the press. Reading the room a plant is pressed in.

Field Note Same species, two rooms One flat by Thursday, one still limp

Two presses, loaded the same afternoon with cuttings from the same patch of garden, can finish a week apart for no reason the plant had any say in. One sat on a shelf near a window that fogged every morning. The other went into a hallway closet with a dehumidifier running for an unrelated reason. Same species, same thickness, same number of blotter changes on paper — and wildly different outcomes, because the air around the press was doing at least half the work the whole time, uninvited and unlogged. Humidity is the variable most guides leave out of their schedules, and it is often the one deciding whether a specimen comes out flat and true-colored or brittle at the veins, or worse, never quite dry at all.

The room is part of the press

A press is often described as a closed system — boards, cardboard, blotters, plant — but it is not sealed, and it should not be. Air moves through the corrugations in the cardboard, carrying moisture out toward whatever surrounds the stack. If that surrounding air is already heavy with its own moisture, it has very little appetite left for what the blotter is trying to hand off. Drying slows, sometimes to a crawl, and a schedule built for a dry climate quietly stops matching reality. If the room is dry instead, the air pulls harder, blotters saturate faster than expected, and a plant that would have taken a week in humid conditions can be crisp in three or four days. Neither condition is wrong. Each just asks for a different rhythm, and pressing by the calendar instead of by the room is where most surprises come from. What a humid room does to a press

In a damp house, a coastal town, or simply a rainy week with the windows shut, blotters that would normally dry out overnight stay cool and slightly limp for two or three days running. The plant senses none of this directly, but the consequence reaches it anyway: moisture lingers longest at the thickest points — a flower head, a fleshy stem base — and that is exactly where discoloration and mildew get their start. A specimen that would flatten cleanly in a dry week can brown at the calyx in a humid one, not because anything was done wrong, but because the paper never got a fair chance to finish its job before the air handed the moisture right back.

The instinct in a humid stretch is usually to add more pressure, hoping it will squeeze the water out faster. It rarely helps much on its own. What actually moves the outcome is changing blotters more often and, where possible, giving the press itself somewhere drier to sit — a room with a fan running, a spot away from a steamy kitchen, anywhere the air isn't already saturated.

Humidity is not weather leaking in from outside. It is the air the plant is still breathing, trapped between two boards.

What a dry room does, and why it isn't automatically better

A heated room in the cold months, or a house with the air conditioning running hard through summer, pulls moisture out fast — often faster than a thin-leaved plant can tolerate without complaint. The surface of a leaf can go crisp while the midrib and the veins beneath it are still slightly pliable, and that mismatch is exactly what produces shattering at the vein when the specimen is finally handled: the outer tissue has nothing left to bend with, so it cracks along the one line where structure was holding it together. Fast is not the same as gentle, and a dry room rewards more frequent checking, not less — the window between "done" and "over-dried and fragile" can close in a single day.

This is also the room where thin, delicate material — grasses, ferns, anything already close to paper-thin — needs the lightest possible touch and the earliest possible removal, since there is little margin between adequately dry and brittle.

Reading the room without a hygrometer

Most collectors never measure humidity with an instrument, and they do not need to. The room usually tells you plainly enough, if you know what to look for.

  • A window that fogs on the inside in the morning, or glass that stays faintly cool and damp to the touch, points to a humid room — expect slower drying and more frequent blotter checks.
  • Salt or sugar in an open shaker that clumps instead of pouring freely is a reliable sign the air is holding more moisture than usual.
  • Laundry hung to dry that still feels damp after several hours suggests the same air working against your press.
  • A room where static crackles at the light switch or where skin feels dry and tight by evening is usually a dry room — expect faster drying and a shorter window before a specimen becomes brittle.

None of these are precise, but precision was never really the goal. The goal is noticing which way the room is leaning, and adjusting the blotter rhythm to match instead of trusting a number written down for a different climate entirely.

Rule of thumb: if a blotter still feels cool and faintly limp after a full day in a room you'd call "dry," don't argue with your hand — trust the blotter over the guess. Something local is holding moisture: a fleshy stem, a closet with poor airflow, a north-facing sill that never quite warms up.

Adjusting blotter frequency by what the room is doing

The interval from a standard schedule is a starting point, not a fixed rule. What the room is actually doing shifts it in either direction, sometimes by days.

When the press makes its own weather

A press tucked into a closed closet or a cabinet with the door shut creates a small climate of its own, regardless of what the rest of the house is doing. The moisture coming off the blotters has nowhere to go, so it lingers right around the press, effectively raising the local humidity even in an otherwise dry home. A small fan aimed at the stack, a slightly open door, or simply moving the press to open shelving solves this more reliably than any change to the blotter schedule. If a specimen from a closet press keeps arriving damp when everything else in the house suggests it should be dry by now, the closet — not the plant, and not the paper — is usually the culprit. For plants that resist flattening even under good conditions, The Species That Refuse to Flatten covers material-specific quirks that compound with a humid setup.

This article describes general drying behavior for reference and does not replace judgment specific to your own climate, room, or plant material. Humidity varies by season, house, and even by shelf, so treat any interval here as a starting point to adjust by what your own blotters tell you.
