Choosing Thread Weight for Old Paper

Thread that is strong enough to hold and gentle enough not to cut a fold that has already gone brittle.

From the bench · March 27, 2026

A spool of 25/3 linen thread looks almost identical to a spool of 18/3 sitting next to it on the same shelf — same waxed sheen, same tidy cross-wind, same faint smell of flax. The difference only shows up once the needle goes through paper that has spent sixty or eighty years getting less flexible instead of more. Reach for the heavier gauge and it does its job of holding the signature together, then quietly saws a slit through the fold over the following months. Reach for the lighter one and it slides through cleanly, then snaps the first time the book gets opened flat on a desk. Thread weight is a small decision in the sequence of a rebind, and one of the easiest to get backwards.

What the Number on the Spool Actually Means

Linen thread is sold under a weight-and-ply notation, something like "25/3" or "18/3" printed small on the label, and the two numbers pull in opposite directions. The first is a weight rating — the higher it climbs, the finer each individual strand is. The second is how many of those strands got twisted together into the thread you're actually holding. A 25/3 is thinner per strand than an 18/3, but plied three ways just the same, the finished 18/3 comes out noticeably heavier and stronger overall. None of that tells you what either one will do to a hundred-year-old fold, though. That part only comes from testing the thread against the specific paper in front of you, not from the label.

Reading the Paper Before Opening the Drawer

Before any thread gets chosen, the paper gets read. Hold a signature up to raking light and the fiber structure shows itself — long and interlocked in an older cotton rag sheet, short and dusty in a wood-pulp sheet that's spent decades acidifying from the inside. Run a thumbnail lightly along an existing crease and listen: a soft give means the fold still has some flex left in it, while a dry crackle means it's closer to snapping than folding. The fold itself is where the paper tells you what it will tolerate a second time, and that reading has to happen before any thread touches it, not after a first pass proves the gauge was wrong.

Where Heavier Thread Starts Cutting

Under normal sewing tension, a heavier thread behaves almost like a straightedge dragged along the fold. On supple paper that resistance barely registers. On paper that has lost most of its flexibility, the sewing holes don't stay round — they elongate, then connect, until the fold has a perforated line running through it that the thread itself put there. The book looks sewn correctly the day it leaves the bench. The failure shows up later, usually the third or fourth time someone opens it past a certain angle.

The thread that holds a signature together on day one can be the same thread that saws it apart by year three.

Where Finer Thread Quietly Fails

The opposite mistake is less visible in the moment and more frustrating later. A thread chosen too light for the job tends to fail at the kettle stitch knots, right where tension concentrates every time the text block flexes open. A snapped thread inside a closed book gives no warning — no fraying edge, no loose page hanging out — until the whole signature separates the next time it's handled. That stress is rarely isolated to the thread alone; it's often the same load a fold already carries from its grain direction, so an under-strength thread and a marginal fold tend to compound each other rather than cancel out.

On the Bench

A 1911 hymnal came in with paper thin enough to see a fingerprint through it under raking light. The standard 18/3 that worked fine on the rest of that season's projects tore a visible line through the second test fold before a single full pass was sewn. Dropping to a doubled 30/3 and waxing it heavily solved the tearing, but it took three ruined scraps of endpaper to find that out — which is the actual argument for testing on scrap first, not for trusting a gauge chosen from memory.

A Rough Match, Not a Formula

The table below reflects general tendencies from bench work, not a rule that applies to every sheet of paper that looks similar on first glance. Two books from the same decade can behave completely differently depending on the mill, the storage conditions, and how many times they've already been opened past their limit.

Paper Condition Typical Thread Needle Note
Supple, minimal browning 25/3 or 30/3 linen #16–18 curved Standard weight holds without extra slack in the fold
Moderate brittleness, slight browning 18/3 linen, waxed #18 curved Wax cuts friction, which matters more once the paper resists
Significant brittleness, short fold life 30/3 doubled #20 fine Two finer passes spread load across more fiber than one heavy pass
Powdery or flaking at the fold Guard before sewing — Sewing alone may not be the right first step at this stage
Mixed condition across one text block Match the weakest signature Varies Sewing to the average treats the strongest fold as the standard

Tension, Wax, and the Needle's Job

Gauge is only half the equation. Hand tension and wax do as much work as the thread itself, especially on paper that's already lost some of its give. A heavily waxed thread moves through a fold with less drag, which matters because drag — not thickness alone — is what tears the paper. Before threading the needle on the actual signature, a few checks earn their five minutes:

  • Pull a short length of the chosen thread taut and note how much give it has before it feels like it's cutting into a fingertip
  • Fold a scrap of similar-weight paper double and see how the gauge sits in the crease under light tension
  • Wax the thread if the paper shows any drag resistance during that scrap test
  • Sew the most damaged or least valuable signature first, so the test is informative rather than costly

None of this replaces judgment built from handling enough books to feel the difference between paper that's merely old and paper that's actually failing. It just narrows the guessing before the needle goes in for real.

A Note, Not a Recommendation

This piece describes tradeoffs observed across a range of bench projects — it isn't a chart to follow without looking at the book in front of you. Paper varies by mill, by decade, and by how it was stored for however many years came before it reached the workbench. The only test that means anything is a scrap pass against the actual signature, not a table on a screen.
