Why Vinyl Fades and Paint Doesn't

A pigment comparison between cut vinyl film and bulletin enamel under direct sun.

May 24, 2026 · Materials

Two banners went up outside a tire shop the same spring, ordered from the same print vendor, mounted on the same south-facing wall a few feet apart. Eighteen months later, the red block lettering on one had gone the chalky pink-orange of a decade-old stop sign, while the blue field behind it still looked close to new. A few blocks over, a hand-lettered enamel panel on a hardware store that closed years ago has faded too — but evenly, the whole sign a shade quieter than it once was, with no single color pulling away from the rest. That split is really the whole story. It isn't vinyl versus paint as materials so much as it's two different ways of carrying color, and how differently each one tends to give that color up under the same sun.

Two Ways to Carry a Color

Cut and printed vinyl carries color in one of two ways. Pre-colored film — the kind used for most cut lettering — has pigment dispersed straight through the PVC sheet, so the color runs its full thickness. Printed vinyl, more common on full-color graphics and banners, lays ink onto the surface of a clear or white film and usually seals it under a laminate. Bulletin enamel works differently again: pigment is ground into an oil or urethane binder and brushed or sprayed on as a solid, opaque coat, with color and protective film effectively the same layer. That distinction — color as a thin printed skin versus color as a load-bearing coat — tends to matter more than which material looks tougher on a spec sheet.

Dye Load vs Pigment Particle

The pigments themselves aren't equally stable, either. Bright printed colors — magentas, some reds, certain purples — often lean on organic pigments or dyes, built from carbon-based molecules whose color comes from a chemical structure that ultraviolet light can break apart fairly directly. Bulletin enamel, by contrast, has leaned for generations on inorganic pigments: iron oxides for reds and browns, titanium dioxide for white, carbon black for black — mineral compounds without that same fragile organic structure for UV to take apart. It's not a rule without exceptions; some outdoor-rated films now use more lightfast pigment sets, and a cheaply cut enamel can fade faster than its reputation suggests. But as a general pattern across what we've seen installed and re-inspected years later, it tends to hold up.

Why the Fade Looks Blotchy on One and Even on the Other

This is where the two materials really part ways visually. Because a printed vinyl graphic usually layers several ink colors to build its full palette, and those inks don't share identical UV resistance, one color channel can drop out while its neighbors barely move — magenta ink fading toward yellow, say, quietly shifting a warm tone toward orange, or a deep blue creeping toward teal as its red component thins first. Enamel rarely has that problem, mostly because a hand-lettered panel usually mixes one pigment per area rather than layering process inks. When it chalks, the whole area chalks together, and the color tends to move in a single direction instead of splitting apart into a shifted version of itself.

Shop Note

On printed vinyl, red and magenta are usually the first colors we notice shifting, sometimes within a season or two of full southern exposure, while the blacks and blues on the same panel still look close to original. It's rarely the whole graphic failing at once — more often it's one ink giving out well ahead of the others.

A Comparison We Watched

Two panels went up the same week outside a corner auto shop a few years back — a printed vinyl banner, and a hand-lettered enamel board mounted just above it, both catching the same afternoon sun. By the second summer, the banner's red pinstripe had faded closer to salmon, and fine print along the bottom, set in a warm orange, had gotten hard to read against its own background from across the lot. The enamel board above it, lettered in a single mixed red, had dulled some — the gloss had gone slightly matte — but the color itself hadn't shifted hue, and the lettering read exactly as clearly as the day it went up. Neither owner had done any maintenance on either sign in that stretch.

Vinyl doesn't fade as one thing getting weaker. It fades as several things getting weaker at different speeds, and the gap between them is what you actually notice.

Laid side by side, the pattern tends to repeat across the jobs we've been able to watch age in place:

A rough order for how printed vinyl tends to fade under sustained direct sun, fastest color to slowest, based on what we've seen repeated across different jobs:

  • Bright reds and magentas, usually the first to visibly shift
  • Oranges and warm yellows, close behind
  • Greens, which vary more depending on the ink set used
  • Blues, generally slower to move
  • Blacks and dark neutrals, typically the last to change

What It Means When You're Choosing a Face

None of this makes vinyl a poor choice — for a seasonal promotion, a banner meant to come down within a year or two anyway, or a full-color photographic graphic that enamel simply can't reproduce, the color-shift timeline is rarely a real problem. It becomes a bigger factor on anything meant to stay up for the long haul: a storefront identity, a fascia sign, a panel nobody plans to revisit for a decade. On that kind of timeline, a single stable pigment applied as a solid coat, the way it's handled in traditional bulletin work (we cover how the steel and coat weather together in Enamel Over Steel), tends to hold its read longer than a printed graphic built from several inks running on different clocks. That stability comes at a real cost gap up front, and it's worth understanding what actually drives that number before choosing either route — we break it down in Pricing a Hand-Lettered Sign.

A note on scope

These are fading patterns we've observed comparing vinyl and enamel signage outdoors, not a lab-tested schedule. Ink formulation, laminate quality, film grade, and exposure angle all vary by manufacturer and installation, and any given sign may age faster or slower than what's described here.
