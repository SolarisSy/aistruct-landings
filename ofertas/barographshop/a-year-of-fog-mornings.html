A Year of Fog Mornings, Counted by Hand

Counting fog by eye instead of instrument turned into the most useful log of the year.

Field Notes · April 18, 2026

The porch thermometer read 54°F at ten minutes to seven on a March morning, and the pressure line in the log sat at 1017.2 millibars, steady, nothing remarkable. Neither number said a word about what the yard actually looked like: the fence line twenty feet off going soft at the edges, the neighbor's chimney gone, the whole block reduced to maybe sixty feet of visible world. Three years of instrument readings never once flagged that. It took a fourth season, and a decision to start marking a single tally on a slip of paper by the back door, before "fog or no fog" became a real column in this log — and it turned out to be one of the more useful ones kept all year.

The Instrument That Wasn't There

A home station covers temperature, pressure, and rainfall well enough on a modest budget, but visibility is a different animal. Airports measure it with transmissometers or forward-scatter sensors, calibrated against a national standard and checked on a schedule this backyard was never going to match — the same gap covered when working out how to calibrate a home barometer without a lab, except visibility has no cheap dial-and-needle equivalent to lean on. A relative-humidity reading gets close some mornings, since fog tends to need air very near saturation, but plenty of mornings sat at ninety-something percent humidity with the fence line perfectly visible, and a few sat lower with the yard fully socked in, depending on how the ground had cooled overnight. Humidity told part of the story. It never told whether fog had actually formed.

A Rule Simple Enough to Keep for a Year

So the method dropped down to the oldest tool on hand: a person standing at the same spot, at roughly the same time, deciding yes or no. That only works if the decision doesn't wobble from one morning to the next, which meant writing the rule down before the first tally went on the page, and sticking to it even on the mornings it felt like cheating to call something "clear" when it still looked a little hazy at the edges.

The Three-Line Rule

Written down before the first entry and never revised mid-year, on purpose:

  • Where: standing on the back step, same spot every morning.
  • When: the first look outside, between 6:30 and 7:00am.
  • What counts: a "fog morning" only if the roofline two properties over has disappeared. Haze, mist that only softens the far fence, or low cloud that still lets the roofline show through, don't count.

What Eighty-One Tally Marks Added Up To

By the end of the twelve months the tally sheet held 81 marks — a little over one fog morning in five. Broken out by season, the pattern wasn't subtle at all, though nothing in three years of temperature and pressure averages had suggested it would be this lopsided.

Fog never shows up in a monthly pressure average or a mean overnight low. It shows up only when someone stands in the same spot at the same hour and decides, out loud, that this is one of the mornings — and then writes it down.

Where One Observer Disagrees With Another

About once every three weeks, someone else in the house looked out at the same time and called it differently — usually on a morning near the edge of the rule, where the roofline was a smudge rather than fully gone. Those disagreements got logged too, in a separate column, rather than resolved by picking a winner on the spot. Over the year they landed on roughly 6 percent of mornings, a number that mostly matters as a ceiling on how much to trust any single day's mark: real, but not exact.

The borderline mornings tended to share a few things in common:

  • Low sun behind the fog, bright enough to read as "clear" from one angle and not from another
  • Wind picking up mid-look, thinning the fog between one glance and the next
  • Fog patchy rather than uniform — thick over the lawn, thin over the street

Reading the Fog Count Against the Rest of the Log

Once the year's tally sat next to the temperature and pressure columns, a few things fell into place that the averages alone had hidden — the same blind spot worked through in the monthly average problem. Fog mornings clustered tightly around nights where the overnight low sat within two or three degrees of the dew point, and the barometer's trend line read steady or slowly rising almost every time, never falling. None of that is a discovery on its own; it is close to textbook radiational cooling. What the tally added was a count, month by month, of how often this yard's particular mix of open sky, damp lawn, and still air actually produced it, instead of leaning on a general sense that fog is common "some mornings in fall."

Starting Your Own Tally, If You Want One

None of this needed an instrument, a subscription, or a spreadsheet more complicated than four columns: date, fog yes or no, clearing time, and a one-line note on what the sky looked like the evening before. A year seems like a reasonable minimum before the pattern says much of anything — a single season repeats too easily by chance. The part worth borrowing isn't the fog rule itself, which will fit one yard and probably nobody else's; it's the habit of writing the rule down first, in language plain enough that a second person standing in the same spot would tend to make the same call, nine mornings out of ten.

A note on what this is: the counts above come from one household's naked-eye log, kept against a fixed rule for a single year. It is not a calibrated visibility measurement of the kind aviation or highway services rely on, and it describes one yard's microclimate rather than a regional pattern. Treat it as a record of what one place tended to do, not a prediction of what any place will do next.
